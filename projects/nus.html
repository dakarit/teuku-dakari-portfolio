FitCheck

A fashion super-app concept that won First Prize at the National University of Singapore’s School of Computing summer workshop. I designed its Virtual Wardrobe and created the design system for our team of four.

The Problem

Young people manage their wardrobes, outfit decisions, shopping, resale, and fashion inspiration across disconnected tools. That makes it hard to use what they already own, make confident fashion decisions, and build a consistent personal style.

FitCheck brings it all into one app: “Your wardrobe, styled, shared, and reworn.” It connects four services: a Virtual Wardrobe, an Outfit Designer, a Marketplace for buying and reselling, and Outfit Sharing.

The service I designed, the Virtual Wardrobe, tackles a narrower problem: people own more clothes than they actually use. Without a clear picture of their closet, they repeat the same few outfits, forget what they’ve bought, and struggle with a basic question: what do I have, and what should I wear?

Context & Constraints

FitCheck was our group project for Designing Interactive Interfaces: UI/UX in the Digital World, an intensive course in the NUS School of Computing Summer Workshop in Singapore. I led our team with Li Ka Kit, Ji Caitong, and Chen Xiangning. I designed the Virtual Wardrobe, created the design system the whole app uses, and took part in all of the team’s shared work: research, the ecosystem map, and testing.

  • We had one month for the entire project, from research to a final showcase, so research and testing had to be small but efficient.
  • Our primary users, teenagers and young adults, range from people who dress for the occasion to people who live and breathe fashion. We had to design for both, plus secondary users like content creators and resellers.
  • Four services had to feel like one continuous app, not four apps stitched together.

Process

1. Research

With one month for the whole project, we ran four semi-structured interviews rather than a larger study, and made each one count by choosing a stratified sample, from people who only dress for occasions to people who decide what to wear every day. Fashion is a form of self-expression, so we wanted qualitative answers over survey data. We asked follow-up questions only where they applied, like digging into thrifting habits only with people who thrift.

We clustered the findings into an affinity diagram and built two deliberately opposite personas: Alex, who only wants to be styled for relevant occasions, and Sam, who wants to make a career out of fashion. Together they cover the whole primary audience and reach into the secondary ones. A journey map for Alex then shaped how the app should flow.

2. Design system

I created the design system for the whole app, built for a calm, photo-first interface where people’s clothes are the focus.

  • Accessible color. Every color is documented with its WCAG contrast ratio, e.g. navy text at 18.3:1 and the accent blue at 5.74:1. The palette stays small so it never competes with the clothes. Blue and white are both neutrals in fashion, so they don’t clash with anything: almost everyone owns something blue, and white lets clothing stand out. Red is reserved for destructive actions like deleting.
  • Tokens in three tiers. Primitives feed semantic tokens, which feed components, so a rebrand is a one-place edit.
  • Glass vs. crisp. Translucent, blurred “glass” is reserved for navigation and controls; content like cards and forms stays solid. It echoes iOS’s Liquid Glass, so the app feels native.
  • One type family. Manrope in six sizes, approachable and clean, with enough weights for a full hierarchy.

3. Designing the Virtual Wardrobe

Each design decision came straight from what interviewees told us.

  • Fast cataloging. “I would not spend hours photographing and entering every item I own.” An AI camera scan fills in an item’s category, brand, size, and condition in seconds. Entering details by hand uses the exact same form, so it’s never a dead end.
  • Deciding what to wear. “I mainly wear the same few clothes because they are easier to find.” Favorite pieces rise to the top of the wardrobe, and any item can be tried on virtually straight from the grid.
  • Surfacing forgotten items. “I sometimes forget that I already own a similar item.” A one-tap “Mark as Worn” builds a wear history, and item cards flag pieces that haven’t been worn in a while.

4. Prototyping and testing

We built FitCheck into an interactive, testable prototype in Figma. Try the prototype (opens in new tab).

Before the final iteration, we ran a lightweight heuristic review alongside an unmoderated usability study in Maze. Given the one-month timeline, the study was about learning usability-testing workflows and catching real problems, not a large-scale research exercise. Participants completed four realistic tasks, like adding an item to the wardrobe, without being shown the intended path, then explored freely so we could see what drew their attention.

Outcome

FitCheck won First Prize at the workshop’s final showcase. View the certificate (PDF)

Building the Maze study pushed us past static screens to define what successful interaction actually means, and showed us where the prototype needed clearer feedback and more complete navigation.
